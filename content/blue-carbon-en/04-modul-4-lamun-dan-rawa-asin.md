# Module 4. Seagrass and salt marsh

Module 4 compares seagrass meadows and tidal salt marshes with mangroves, two blue carbon ecosystems that have no trees and store nearly all of their carbon below the surface. After this module, participants can build a comparison table of the three ecosystems (features, distribution, carbon stocks from the books, and measurement methods) and calculate the biomass carbon of seagrass and salt marsh grasses from simple field data.

After completing this module, participants are able to:

## Content

### 4.1 Seagrass meadows: features, carbon, and measurement

Seagrasses are flowering plants that live submerged in the sea. Seagrasses are not seaweed (algae) because they have roots, rhizomes, leaves, flowers, and seeds. The number of species is small. Hogarth writes about 58 species in 12 genera (H chapter 1), while the guidebook writes about 60 species in four families (BC chapter 4). Participants write down the number they use in the comparison table.

**Distribution and living requirements.** Unlike mangroves, which are limited to the tropics and subtropics, seagrasses occur on every continent except Antarctica (BC chapter 4; H chapter 1). Seagrasses generally need more than 11% of surface light, so water turbidity limits the depth at which they grow. The greatest depth recorded is about 90 m (H chapter 3).

**Body form.** The seagrass stem is a rhizome that creeps within the sediment, and its upright shoots carry leaves above and roots below. Usually 50 to 60% of seagrass biomass lies below the sediment surface (H chapter 3). The leaves have no stomata, and seagrasses use bicarbonate in seawater as their carbon source (H chapter 3).

**Biomass and carbon.** Seagrass biomass is usually less than 5 tonnes dry weight per hectare (H chapter 8), far below mangroves. Almost all of its carbon is in the sediment. BC chapter 3 states that the soil share of carbon is often more than 98% of the stock of a seagrass ecosystem. Two numbers need to be read together: 50 to 60% of living biomass is below the sediment, but living roots and rhizomes are only 0.3% of all belowground organic carbon (BC chapter 4). The denominators of the two numbers differ, so they do not contradict each other.

Numbers from the books for seagrass:

- World mean soil carbon stock to 1 m is 108 Mg C/ha, range 10 to 829, or 396 Mg CO2e/ha (BC Table 1.2).
- For the Indo-Pacific region, BC Table 4.8 (Fourqurean et al. 2012) records living biomass of 0.61 ± 0.26 Mg C/ha (n = 47) and soil carbon of 23.6 ± 8.3 Mg C/ha (n = 8). That soil value is about a quarter of the world mean, so the world Tier 1 may be too high for this region. The number of soil samples is only eight.
- About 50% of seagrass sediment carbon comes from outside the meadow (allochthonous) (BC chapter 4).
- The rate of seagrass loss rose from a median of 0.9% per year before 1940 to 7% per year since 1990 (BC chapter 4).

**How to measure.** Seagrass that is exposed at low tide is worked on foot, while seagrass that is always submerged is worked by snorkel or diving gear. Plots are small, 0.25 to 1 m². Strata are made by water depth interval. Biomass is measured at the peak and repeated at the same time each year. A tube 10 to 25 cm in diameter is pressed into the sediment to about 40 cm. Its contents are washed, separated into leaves and living root-rhizomes, dried at 60 °C for about 72 hours, then weighed. The seagrass carbon conversion factor is 0.34 (BC chapter 4). Epiphytes are removed by scraping, not by acid, if epiphyte carbon is to be counted.

Seagrass soil cores are taken with a piston-driven PVC tube that is hammered in, carried upright, and then sectioned more finely in the top 50 cm (for example every 5 cm). The soil carbon calculation is the same as in Module 7.

The tube cross-section area and conversion to hectares use the following two formulas.

```latex
B = \frac{W}{\pi (d/2)^2 \times 10^{-4}} \quad \text{(g/m}^2\text{)}
```

```latex
C\ (\text{Mg C/ha}) = B \times f \times 0.01
```

Here W is dry weight (g), d is tube diameter (cm), B is biomass (g/m²), and f is the carbon conversion factor (0.34 for seagrass). The factor 0.01 comes from 1 g/m² = 0.01 Mg/ha. If biomass is in kg/m², the factor is 10.

**Worked example (illustration).** One tube 15 cm in diameter yields seagrass leaves with a dry weight of 8.5 g.

1. Cross-section area = π × 7.5² = 176.7 cm² = 0.017671 m².
2. Leaf biomass = 8.5 ÷ 0.017671 = 481.0 g/m².
3. Carbon = 481.0 × 0.34 = 163.5 g C/m² = 0.1635 kg C/m².
4. Per hectare = 163.5 × 0.01 = 1.64 Mg C/ha (the same as 0.1635 × 10).

This figure of 1.64 Mg C/ha comes from one tube and only the leaf part. It is not a meadow mean. For comparison, living biomass of all parts in the Indo-Pacific averages 0.61 Mg C/ha (BC Table 4.8). The soil stores tens of times more.

**Summary 4.1**

- Seagrass is a submerged flowering plant whose distribution is limited by light, not latitude.
- Seagrass carbon is almost entirely in the sediment, and about half of it comes from outside the meadow.
- Measurement uses 0.25 to 1 m² plots, depth strata, the conversion factor 0.34, and soil cores from a piston tube.

### 4.2 Salt marsh and comparing the three ecosystems

A tidal salt marsh is a stretch of low plants in the upper part of the tidal zone that is regularly flooded by the high tide. The plants are salt-tolerant grasses, sedges, herbs, and low shrubs, without trees (BC chapter 4). Unlike mangroves and seagrasses, salt marsh halophytes are not all characteristic of tidal settings.

**Distribution.** Salt marshes are most common in temperate, subarctic, and arctic regions. In the humid tropics the same places are covered by mangroves, and in the dry tropics they become salt flats (BC chapter 4; H chapter 9). Global salt marsh area figures are not used in this module.

**Zonation.** Marshes are arranged by soil elevation: low marsh, mid marsh, high marsh, and brackish marsh at the landward edge. This division is used as the basis for strata because each zone stores carbon in a different way. Low and mid marsh are dominated by belowground carbon, while aboveground biomass becomes significant only in high marsh (BC chapter 4).

**Carbon.** The ratio of roots to aboveground parts ranges from 1.4 to 50 (BC chapter 3). Belowground carbon is usually 65 to 95% of the ecosystem stock (BC chapter 4), while BC chapter 3 writes that the soil share of carbon is often more than 98%. The two numbers come from different chapters and sources, so participants record which number they use. The world mean soil carbon stock to 1 m is 255 Mg C/ha, range 16 to 623, or 935 Mg CO2e/ha (BC Table 1.2). Losses come among other things from dikes and drainage for agriculture, salt ponds and shrimp ponds, and changes in drainage. North America has lost about 50% since the arrival of European settlers, and China 50% since the 1950s (BC chapter 4). Hogarth judges salt marsh productivity to be equivalent to mangroves, with more carbon retained and less carried out (H chapter 9).

**How to measure.** Salt marsh biomass changes with the season, so it is measured at the peak (usually mid to late summer in cold regions) and repeated in the same season. Strata are bands parallel to the shoreline or tidal creeks. BC recommends plots of about 20 × 50 m per stratum with at least five to six random small quadrats (BC chapter 4). For grasses, sedges, and herbs, quadrats of 30 × 30 cm are harvested, dried at 60 °C for about 72 hours, and weighed. The conversion factor is 0.45. Roots and rhizomes are taken with a core 10 cm in diameter to 1 m, washed on a 1 mm sieve, dried, and weighed, with a factor of 0.34. Shrubs are measured like dwarf mangrove with a factor of 0.46 to 0.50.

**Worked example (illustration).** One 30 × 30 cm quadrat yields 52 g of dry grass. One root core 10 cm in diameter to 1 m yields 120 g of dry living roots and rhizomes.

1. Grass: quadrat area = 0.3 × 0.3 = 0.09 m². Biomass = 52 ÷ 0.09 = 577.8 g/m². Carbon = 577.8 × 0.45 = 260.0 g C/m² = 2.60 Mg C/ha.
2. Roots: core area = π × 5² = 78.54 cm² = 0.007854 m². Biomass = 120 ÷ 0.007854 = 15,279 g/m². Carbon = 15,279 × 0.34 = 5,195 g C/m² = 51.95 Mg C/ha.
3. Ratio of roots to aboveground = 15,279 ÷ 577.8 = 26.4. This value is within the range 1.4 to 50 (BC chapter 3).
4. Vegetation carbon = 2.60 + 51.95 = 54.55 Mg C/ha, and 95% of it is in the roots.

The figure of 54.55 Mg C/ha is the vegetation carbon from one quadrat and one core, not yet including soil. Compared with the mean soil of 255 Mg C/ha, living biomass is still not the main pool.

**Differences and limitations in the books' numbers.** The following must be recorded on the "Comparison" sheet.

- Number of seagrass species: about 58 species in 12 genera (H chapter 1) or about 60 species in four families (BC chapter 4).
- Belowground carbon share in salt marsh: 65 to 95% (BC chapter 4) or more than 98% (BC chapter 3). Write the number used and the reason.
- The CO2e column in BC Table 1.2 for mangrove is 1,415, whereas 386 × 3.67 = 1,416.6. This small difference comes from rounding. Salt marsh (255 × 3.67 = 935.9) and seagrass (108 × 3.67 = 396.4) match the table.
- The Tier 1 example in BC uses 386 Mg C/ha for "biomass and 1 m of soil" on a 564 ha site, whereas the title of Table 1.2 calls that value the soil carbon stock to 1 m. Use 386 as soil carbon only.
- Hogarth writes carbon accumulation rates of 22.6 (mangrove) and 13.8 (seagrass) t/ha/year. The numbers usually quoted from the source are 226 and 138 g C/m²/year, which is 2.26 and 1.38 t/ha/year. This comparison note is outside the books, so check the original source (McLeod et al. 2011) before quoting.

**Summary 4.2**

- Salt marsh is a treeless ecosystem in the upper tidal zone, most common in temperate regions.
- Salt marsh carbon is mainly in roots and soil, and the share figures differ between chapters of the guidebook.
- Tier 1 soil to 1 m: mangrove 386, salt marsh 255, seagrass 108 Mg C/ha, with very wide ranges.

## Self-study exercises

Work on the "Comparison" sheet in the workbook. Write the formulas used in the cells, not just the results. Numbers labeled illustration were made up for practice. Tier 1 numbers come from BC Table 1.2.

**Problem 1 (easy).** The Sample Forest is 564 ha. Suppose the entire area were calculated only with Tier 1 for the three ecosystems: mangrove 386, salt marsh 255, and seagrass 108 Mg C/ha. Calculate the soil stock (Mg C) and the CO2e equivalent (multiply by 3.67) for each.

**Problem 2 (easy).** A seagrass core tube 10 cm in diameter (illustration) yields 4.2 g of dry leaves and 5.4 g of dry living root-rhizomes. With a factor of 0.34, calculate leaf carbon, root-rhizome carbon, and the total in Mg C/ha, and the share belowground in the sediment.

**Problem 3 (medium, Sample Forest data).** The soil carbon stock to 1 m in the Sample Forest is 380 (stratum A, 180 ha), 495 (stratum B, 264 ha), and 430 Mg C/ha (stratum C, 120 ha). Calculate the soil stock of each stratum, the site total, and the weighted mean per hectare. Compare with the mangrove Tier 1 of 386 Mg C/ha and explain one reason for the difference.

**Problem 4 (medium).** One 30 × 30 cm quadrat in a salt marsh (illustration) yields 31 g of dry grass. One core 10 cm in diameter to 1 m yields 86 g of dry living roots and rhizomes. Calculate grass carbon and root carbon (Mg C/ha), the ratio of roots to aboveground, and the root share of vegetation carbon. Is the ratio within the book's range?

**Problem 5 (harder).** A 140 ha seagrass meadow (illustration) is divided into three depth strata. Soil data to 1 m:

```csv
stratum,depth_m,area_ha,soil_carbon_MgC_per_ha
L1,0-2,40,62
L2,2-5,70,48
L3,5-10,30,31
```

Calculate the total stock (Mg C) and the weighted mean per hectare. Compare with the seagrass Tier 1 (108 Mg C/ha) and the Indo-Pacific value of 23.6 Mg C/ha (BC Table 4.8). Write which number you would use in a report and why.

### Answer key

**Problem 1.** Mangrove: 386 × 564 = 217,704 Mg C, CO2e = 217,704 × 3.67 = 798,974 Mg. Salt marsh: 255 × 564 = 143,820 Mg C, CO2e = 527,819 Mg. Seagrass: 108 × 564 = 60,912 Mg C, CO2e = 223,547 Mg. If the BC CO2e column is used (1,415; 935; 396) the results are 798,060; 527,340; 223,344 Mg, a difference of about 0.1% due to rounding. Record which column is used.

**Problem 2.** Tube area = π × 5² = 78.54 cm² = 0.007854 m². Leaves: 4.2 ÷ 0.007854 = 534.8 g/m²; × 0.34 = 181.8 g C/m² = 1.82 Mg C/ha. Root-rhizomes: 5.4 ÷ 0.007854 = 687.5 g/m²; × 0.34 = 233.8 g C/m² = 2.34 Mg C/ha. Total 4.16 Mg C/ha. Share below the sediment = 2.34 ÷ 4.16 = 56%, within the range of 50 to 60% (H chapter 3).

**Problem 3.** A: 380 × 180 = 68,400. B: 495 × 264 = 130,680. C: 430 × 120 = 51,600. Total 250,680 Mg C. Weighted mean = 250,680 ÷ 564 = 444.5 Mg C/ha. Tier 1 gives 386 × 564 = 217,704 Mg C, so the field data are higher by 32,976 Mg C (15.1%). A usable reason: 386 is a world mean with a range of 55 to 1,376 (BC Table 1.2), so a single site may reasonably deviate from it. Field numbers are more trusted than Tier 1.

**Problem 4.** Grass: 31 ÷ 0.09 = 344.4 g/m²; × 0.45 = 155.0 g C/m² = 1.55 Mg C/ha. Roots: 86 ÷ 0.007854 = 10,950 g/m²; × 0.34 = 3,723 g C/m² = 37.23 Mg C/ha. Ratio of roots to aboveground = 10,950 ÷ 344.4 = 31.8, within the range 1.4 to 50 (BC chapter 3). Vegetation carbon = 38.78 Mg C/ha, and roots contribute 96%.

**Problem 5.** L1: 40 × 62 = 2,480. L2: 70 × 48 = 3,360. L3: 30 × 31 = 930. Total 6,770 Mg C. Weighted mean = 6,770 ÷ 140 = 48.4 Mg C/ha. The plain mean of the three strata (47.0) must not be used because the stratum areas differ. Tier 1 gives 108 × 140 = 15,120 Mg C, which is 2.2 times the measured stock. The figure of 48.4 is closer to the Indo-Pacific value of 23.6 than to 108, but the Indo-Pacific data come from only eight samples. For a report, use 48.4 (your own data) and mention Tier 1 only as a comparison.

## Find the error

The two report excerpts below look tidy but contain errors. Find the errors before reading the key.

**Report A (seagrass, illustration).** "One tube 15 cm in diameter yields 8.5 g of dry seagrass leaves. Cross-section area = 3.14 × 15² = 706.5 cm² = 0.07065 m². Biomass = 8.5 ÷ 0.07065 = 120.3 g/m². Carbon = 120.3 × 0.34 = 40.9 g C/m² = 0.41 Mg C/ha. So the biomass carbon of seagrass leaves is only 0.41 Mg C/ha."

**Report B (salt marsh, illustration).** "A 120 ha salt marsh site. Tier 1 soil stock = 255 × 120 = 30,600 Mg CO2e. Roots and rhizomes: 15,279 g/m² × 0.45 = 6,876 g C/m² = 68.8 Mg C/ha."

### Key

**Report A.** The error: the 15 cm diameter was used as the radius. The area formula needs the radius, which is 7.5 cm. The correct area is 176.7 cm² (not 706.5), so the correct biomass is 481.0 g/m², carbon 163.5 g C/m², and the final result 1.64 Mg C/ha. The report gives a value a quarter of the correct one. How to detect it: a 10 to 25 cm tube means a cross-section area of about 80 to 490 cm². An area of 706.5 cm² is equivalent to a tube 30 cm in diameter, outside the range of the tool. Also check by recalculating π × (d ÷ 2)² separately.

**Report B.** There are two errors.

1. The figure 255 × 120 = 30,600 is Mg C, not Mg CO2e. The CO2e equivalent = 30,600 × 3.67 = 112,302 Mg. How to detect it: the CO2e column of BC Table 1.2 for salt marsh is 935, about 3.7 times 255. A result labeled CO2e that is almost the same as the carbon result is certainly wrong.
2. The factor 0.45 was used for roots and rhizomes. The factor 0.45 is for grass and litter, while salt marsh roots and rhizomes use 0.34 (BC chapter 4). The correct result: 15,279 × 0.34 = 5,195 g C/m² = 51.95 Mg C/ha. The report overstates by 16.8 Mg C/ha (32%). How to detect it: match the conversion factor of each component against the conversion factor table before calculating.

## The role of AI and example prompts

AI can help draft Excel formulas, explain terms, and check the order of calculations. AI does not replace the books and your own calculation. Every number from AI must be matched against the books (BC, KD, H) or recalculated by hand.

**Prompt 1: drafting formulas.**

```markdown
I have seagrass core-tube data in Excel: column A tube diameter (cm), column B leaf dry weight (g). Write formulas in column C for tube area (m2), D for biomass (g/m2), E for carbon (Mg C/ha) with a conversion factor of 0.34. Explain the units at each step.
```

Check: enter A2 = 15 and B2 = 8.5, then match E2 with the hand calculation in section 4.1, which is 1.64 Mg C/ha. If different, check the area unit and the 0.01 factor.

**Prompt 2: explaining terms.**

```markdown
Explain the difference between allochthonous and autochthonous carbon in seagrass meadow sediment in three sentences, for blue carbon course participants who are not ecologists.
```

Check: match the explanation with BC chapter 4, which is that about 50% of seagrass sediment carbon comes from outside the meadow. If the AI states another number, treat it as unverified.

**Prompt 3: checking a calculation.**

```markdown
Check this calculation step by step and tell me if there is any unit or conversion factor error: salt marsh grass 52 g dry from a 30 x 30 cm quadrat, factor 0.45, result 2.6 Mg C/ha.
```

Check: calculate it yourself, 52 ÷ 0.09 × 0.45 × 0.01 = 2.60. Match the factor 0.45 against the conversion factor table.

**Example of an AI answer that can be wrong.** For Prompt 1, the AI writes: area in C2 = `=PI()*A2^2/10000`, biomass in D2 = `=B2/C2`, and carbon in E2 = `=D2*0.34*10`. The formulas look tidy and the cells display numbers. There are two errors: A2 is used as the radius (it should be `PI()*(A2/2)^2/10000`), and D2 in g/m² is multiplied by 0.34 and then 10, whereas g/m² becomes Mg/ha with a factor of 0.01. With A2 = 15 and B2 = 8.5, the value of E2 becomes 408.9 Mg C/ha, far from 1.64. How to catch it: calculate one data row by hand and compare. A leaf biomass carbon of 408.9 Mg C/ha is also implausible because it exceeds the world mean seagrass soil carbon (108 Mg C/ha) and seagrass biomass is usually less than 5 t dry weight/ha (H chapter 8).

## Excel notes: Mac and Windows

The following table is used for problems 1 to 5. The formula examples assume data in row 2 onward with columns as named in the Step column. In Excel and Google Sheets in Indonesian, function names can differ and the argument separator is usually a semicolon, not a comma. The examples here use commas. Replace them with semicolons if Excel rejects a formula, and check function names through Formulas > Insert Function.

| Step | Mac | Windows | Google Sheets |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tube area (m²) from diameter in cm in A2 | `=PI()*(A2/2)^2/10000` | `=PI()*(A2/2)^2/10000` | `=PI()*(A2/2)^2/10000` |
| Biomass g/m² (dry weight g in B2, area in C2) | `=B2/C2` | `=B2/C2` | `=B2/C2` |
| Carbon Mg C/ha from g/m² (D2) and factor 0.34 | `=D2*0.34*0.01` | `=D2*0.34*0.01` | `=D2*0.34*0.01` |
| Stock per stratum (area C2 × carbon D2) | `=C2*D2` | `=C2*D2` | `=C2*D2` |
| Fill formulas down | Select the cell, press Cmd+D | Select the cell, press Ctrl+D | Select the cell, press Ctrl+D (Windows) or Cmd+D (Mac) |
| Sum | `=SUM(E2:E4)` | `=SUM(E2:E4)` | `=SUM(E2:E4)` |
| Area-weighted mean | `=SUMPRODUCT(C2:C4,D2:D4)/SUM(C2:C4)` | `=SUMPRODUCT(C2:C4,D2:D4)/SUM(C2:C4)` | `=SUMPRODUCT(C2:C4,D2:D4)/SUM(C2:C4)` |
| Check the root:aboveground ratio range (F2) | `=IF(F2>=1.4,IF(F2<=50,"within range","outside"),"outside")` | same | same |
| Lock a cell reference (for example the factor in `$H$1`) | Cmd+T on the cell being edited | F4 on the cell being edited | Type the dollar sign manually |
| Two-decimal format | Cmd+1 > Number | Ctrl+1 > Number | Format > Number > Custom number format |
| Split pasted CSV data | Data > Text to Columns | Data > Text to Columns | Data > Split text to columns |

A plain mean of strata (AVERAGE) is not the same as an area-weighted mean. In problems 3 and 5, use SUMPRODUCT.
