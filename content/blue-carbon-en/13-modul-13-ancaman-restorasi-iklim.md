# Module 13. Threats, restoration, climate

## Module overview

Module 13 covers four things: the benefits of mangroves to people, threats to mangroves and seagrass, management and restoration, and the effect of climate change on coastal carbon storage. This module closes the course. Participants calculate a few simple examples from book numbers, then submit the final project and a short reflection.

## Learning objectives

After this module, participants are able to:

1. name five groups of benefits mangroves give to people and explain why their economic value is hard to calculate;
2. identify five causes of mangrove loss (overexploitation, aquaculture ponds, pollution, changes in hydrology, storms) and four threats to seagrass (dredging, sediment, excess nutrients, disease), and link them to the loss of carbon storage;
3. calculate remaining area from an annual loss rate by compounding, then compare it with a simple calculation;
4. distinguish planting from restoration and prepare a site assessment checklist before planting;
5. calculate the difference between sea level rise and change in soil surface height, and conclude whether a stratum is able to keep pace;
6. judge the share of mangrove loss that can be prevented, distinguishing direct human causes from climate causes.

## Content

### 13.1 Benefits of and threats to mangroves

Coastal communities use mangroves for building timber, firewood, charcoal, animal fodder, and tanning materials. Nipa is used for roofing, weaving, food, and sap. Fisheries also depend on mangroves: KD states that up to 75% of commercial fish species in the tropics spend part of their life cycle in mangroves (KD).

Coastal protection is a benefit that is often undervalued. According to Hogarth, depending on the species, size, and density of the trees, mangrove forest can reduce wind speed by more than 50%, dampen waves by up to 66% after passing through 100 m of forest, and lower storm wave height by 40 to 50 cm for every kilometre of forest (H 11.1.1). Their economic value is hard to calculate because many benefits are not traded. A global study valued mangroves and tidal marshes at about US$14,500 per hectare per year (2014 prices; H chapter 11).

**Worked example 1: wave attenuation (illustration).** A wave 1.5 m high (illustration) enters a mangrove forest. What is its height after 100 m, using Hogarth's numbers?

1. Attenuation up to 66%, so the wave that remains is at least 100% − 66% = 34%.
2. Height after 100 m: 1.5 × (1 − 0.66) = 0.51 m. Because the book figure is an upper bound ("up to 66%"), the true height can be larger, at most 1.5 m if the forest does not attenuate.

**Worked example 2: storm waves (illustration).** A storm wave 2.0 m high (illustration) passes through a forest 1.5 km deep. Book figure: a decrease of 40 to 50 cm per kilometre. Assuming the decrease applies linearly (this assumption is outside the book): total decrease 0.4 × 1.5 = 0.60 m to 0.5 × 1.5 = 0.75 m, so the wave height behind the forest is 1.25 to 1.40 m.

On economic value: US$14,500 per hectare per year × 564 ha = US$8,178,000 per year. This number only shows the order of magnitude if the world figure is borrowed. The world mean figure must not be used as the Sample Forest's value. For a report, write it as a range and state its limits.

**Threats.** World mangrove area decreased by 19% between 1980 and 2005. The rate of loss has fallen: an average of 1.04% per year in the 1980s, 0.72% in the 1990s, and 0.66% in 2000–2005. Almost all of it is caused by people (H chapter 11). The main causes:

| Cause | Facts from the book | Reference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Overexploitation | Sabah, Malaysia: about 70,000 ha felled for wood chips in 1971–1986, far exceeding the forest's capacity to recover | H 11.1.3 |
| Shrimp ponds | In the eight countries with the most mangroves, loss of 51.9% since the 1970s; about 544,000 ha, or 28% of that loss, due to ponds. One hectare of semi-intensive pond needs the support of 9.6 to 180 ha of mangrove. The excavated mud becomes increasingly acidic | H 11.1.3.3 |
| Pollution | Oil clogs lenticels; recovery from a large spill can take 20 to 30 years | H 11.1.3.4 |
| Changes in hydrology | Indus delta: river flow fell from about 150 to often less than 30 million acre-feet per year, sediment fell to a fifth, mangrove area fell by more than half; grazing by about 26,000 livestock played a part | H 11.1.3.5 |
| Storms | Hurricane Andrew (1992) severely damaged about 150 km² of mangrove in Florida; peat collapsed and the soil surface fell | H 11.1.3 |

From the carbon side, each threat above affects a different pool. Logging reduces aboveground biomass and roots. Soil that is excavated and exposed to air becomes acidic and, according to BC chapter 1, releases its carbon as CO2. The calculation of its emissions follows Module 11.

**Worked example 3: remaining area at a constant rate (illustration).** The 564 ha Sample Forest loses area at a rate of 0.66% per year (the 2000–2005 world rate, H chapter 11, borrowed as an illustration). What is its area after 10 years?

```latex
A_n = A_0 \times (1 - r)^n
```

1. A0 = 564 ha, r = 0.0066, n = 10.
2. (1 − 0.0066)^10 = 0.99340^10 = 0.93593.
3. A10 = 564 × 0.93593 = 527.9 ha.
4. Area lost = 564 − 527.9 = 36.1 ha.
5. Comparison: the simple calculation 564 × 0.0066 × 10 = 37.2 ha. The difference of 1.1 ha arises because the annual rate is applied to an ever-smaller area.

For other cases, the Sample Forest at a rate of 1% per year becomes 510.1 ha and at 2% per year 460.8 ha in 10 years (illustrative calculation).

**Worked example 4: ponds and the forest that supports them.** One hectare of semi-intensive pond needs 9.6 to 180 ha of mangrove forest (H 11.1.3.3). For a 10 ha pond: 10 × 9.6 = 96 ha to 10 × 180 = 1,800 ha of forest. Conversely, a 564 ha forest supports at most 564 ÷ 9.6 = 58.75 ha of pond (lower bound of the requirement) and at least 564 ÷ 180 = 3.13 ha (upper bound).

### 13.2 Sustainable management and restoration

Sustainable management is possible. The Matang forest in western Malaysia has been managed for more than a hundred years. Its area is more than 40,000 ha, and about 30,000 ha of it is production forest for charcoal. The forest is divided into blocks that are felled in rotation on a 30-year cycle, a 3 m wide strip is left along riverbanks to prevent erosion, planting of Rhizophora apiculata is done where needed, and the stand is thinned at about 15 and 20 years of age. Timber yield fell from about 296 to 158, then 136 tonnes per hectare, so this management may not yet be fully sustainable. Timber is only about 12% of the area's economic value; fisheries in the surrounding waters are far greater (H 11.1.3).

**Worked example 5: the Matang forest.**

1. Area felled per year = 30,000 ha ÷ 30 years = 1,000 ha per year, the same as the book figure (about 1,000 ha).
2. Yield decline from the original stand to the last figure: 296 − 136 = 160 tonnes per hectare.
3. Percentage: 160 ÷ 296 = 54.1%. From 296 to 158: 46.6%; from 158 to 136: 13.9%.

Another example, gei wai aquaculture in China: mangroves are left to grow in the middle of the pond, shrimp seed and nutrients enter with the tide through a sluice gate, the yield is less than 500 kg per hectare per year, costs are small, and the mangroves stay intact (H 11.1.3).

**Restoration.** Propagules are easy to collect and plant, but many plantings fail. If a site is not covered by mangroves, there is usually a reason: the soil elevation, hydrology, salinity, or soil is unsuitable. Lessons from Hogarth (H 11.1.4):

- Assess the site first. Fixing the hydrology, for example by creating new tidal channels, is often more decisive than planting.
- Choose species that suit the place. Rhizophora is often chosen because its propagules are easy to get, although in natural forest it grows in the middle to upper shore. For the seaward front, Avicennia or Sonneratia is more suitable.
- Seedling survival in the early years varies greatly, from less than 10% to about 90%. Monitor until the trees are old enough to fruit.
- Three restoration targets: area (Area), biodiversity (Biodiversity), and connectivity between parts of the forest (Connectance).

**Worked example 6: seedling survival (illustration).** A site is planted with 10,000 seedlings (illustration). At 10% survival, 10,000 × 0.10 = 1,000 seedlings remain. At 90%, 10,000 × 0.90 = 9,000 seedlings remain. This ninefold difference shows why the number of seedlings planted is not a measure of success; what is measured is how many survive to fruiting.

**Relation to carbon.** Soil takes a long time to accumulate carbon. A newly restored site may not have a significant soil carbon store for the first few years (note in Module 7; BC chapter 3; H 11.1.4). So do not enter the mature-forest soil stock, such as 495 Mg C/ha in stratum B of the Sample Forest, as an estimate for a newly planted restoration site.

### 13.3 Seagrass threats and climate change

**Seagrass.** The main threats come from people: dredging of shipping channels destroys seagrass meadows directly; coastal development and land erosion add sediment so light is reduced; agricultural and household waste add nutrients and trigger algal blooms. Disease in the mid-20th century wiped out more than 90% of Zostera cover in northern Europe (H 11.2). The guidebook records that the rate of seagrass loss rose from a median of 0.9% per year before 1940 to 7% per year since 1990 (BC chapter 4; H 12.5).

**Worked example 7: seagrass at two rates (illustration).** A 1,000 ha seagrass meadow (illustration) declines for 10 years.

1. Rate 0.9% per year: 1,000 × (1 − 0.009)^10 = 1,000 × 0.9136 = 913.6 ha.
2. Rate 7% per year: 1,000 × (1 − 0.07)^10 = 1,000 × 0.4840 = 484.0 ha.
3. At a rate of 7%, less than half the area remains after 10 years.

**Climate change** (H chapter 12):

- Atmospheric CO2 rose from about 280 ppm (late 18th century) to 391 ppm (2011), an increase of 111 ppm or 39.6%. Mangroves are more water-efficient at high CO2, but the effect on growth is uncertain and differs between species; Hogarth judges the impact to be smaller than other factors. Ocean pH has already fallen by about 0.1.
- Mean global temperature rose about 0.85 °C in a hundred years. Mangrove distribution shifts toward the poles. The northern limit of Avicennia germinans on the east coast of the United States is tied to the number of days with temperatures below −4 °C, so expansion can be sudden. Models estimate a shift of about 2 degrees of latitude, more than 200 km, in 80 years (one degree of latitude is about 111 km, outside the book: 2 × 111 = 222 km).
- Sea level rose an average of 3.4 mm per year in 1993–2007 and could rise up to 82 cm by the end of this century. Mangroves persist by retreating landward (if not blocked by roads, buildings, or farmland) or by raising their own soil surface. Monitoring shows soil surface rise of 0.7 to 20.8 mm per year, in many places reduced by subsidence of 0 to 20.8 mm per year. Not all forests are able to keep pace with sea level rise (H 12.3).
- Healthy forest usually recovers from disturbance. In forest already stressed by other causes, gaps can become permanent, so climate impacts can appear suddenly.
- Mangroves are lost at about 1 to 2% per year and seagrass at up to 7% per year. Mangrove loss directly caused by climate change is estimated to be only 10 to 20% of the loss from land clearing and other human activities. So most of the loss can still be prevented through protection and management (H chapter 12).

**Worked example 8: the balance against sea level.** The change in soil surface height measured with SET is compared with sea level rise. Both rates are in mm per year. The shallow subsidence formula from Module 11:

```latex
\text{shallow subsidence} = \text{accretion} - \text{surface height change (SET)}
```

```latex
\text{gap} = \text{sea level rate} - \text{surface height change (SET)}
```

A positive gap means the soil surface is falling behind sea level. The following data were designed for practice; what is used from the book is only the range 0.7–20.8 mm per year.

#### Sample Forest data: accretion and soil surface height change (illustration)

```csv
stratum,accretion_mm_per_year,SET_height_change_mm_per_year
A,6.0,2.5
B,5.0,4.2
C,3.0,1.5
```

Stratum A, with sea level at 3.4 mm per year (mean 1993–2007):

1. Shallow subsidence = 6.0 − 2.5 = 3.5 mm per year.
2. Gap = 3.4 − 2.5 = 0.9 mm per year, so the soil surface falls behind by 0.9 mm per year.
3. Over 80 years: 0.9 × 80 = 72 mm.
4. In the 82 cm scenario: if that rise occurs in about 90 years (this period is an assumption, outside the book), the rate is 820 ÷ 90 = 9.1 mm per year, and the stratum A gap becomes 9.1 − 2.5 = 6.6 mm per year, or 528 mm in 80 years.

**Worked example 9: the share of loss related to climate.** If total mangrove loss is 1 to 2% per year and the climate share is 10 to 20% of that, loss due to climate is 0.10 × 1% = 0.1% to 0.20 × 2% = 0.4% per year. In the Sample Forest: 564 × 0.001 = 0.56 ha to 564 × 0.004 = 2.26 ha per year, whereas total loss at 1–2% is 5.64 to 11.28 ha per year (illustration).

**Differences between books.** For this module there are no differences in numbers between books that need choosing. If participants use a loss rate, write down the book, the period, and whether the rate is for world mangroves, a region, or seagrass.

## Self-study exercises

Work in Excel or Google Sheets. Problems 2, 4, and 5 use the Sample Forest (564 ha: stratum A 180 ha, B 264 ha, C 120 ha). Write the book numbers used with their references.

1. **Rotational production forest (easy, illustration).** A charcoal production forest of 900 ha (illustration) is felled in rotation on a 30-year cycle. (a) How many hectares are felled per year? (b) How many if the cycle is shortened to 20 years? (c) In the Matang forest, timber yield fell from 296 to 158 tonnes per hectare; calculate the decline as a percentage.
2. **Remaining area (medium).** The Sample Forest loses area at rates of 1% and 2% per year. Calculate the area after 15 years and the area lost for each rate, using the compound formula.
3. **Aquaculture ponds (medium).** A semi-intensive pond of 8 ha needs the support of 9.6 to 180 ha of mangrove per hectare of pond (H 11.1.3.3). (a) How many hectares of forest are needed? (b) What are the most and the fewest hectares of pond that the 564 ha Sample Forest can support?
4. **Sea level balance (medium-hard).** Using the table "Sample Forest data: accretion and soil surface height change (illustration)" in section 13.3, calculate for strata B and C: shallow subsidence, the gap relative to sea level of 3.4 mm per year, and the gap relative to 9.1 mm per year. Also calculate the cumulative 80-year gap (mm), then write one sentence on which stratum is the most vulnerable.
5. **Climate share (hard, illustration).** (a) With total loss of 1 to 2% per year and a climate share of 10 to 20%, calculate the Sample Forest area lost to climate in 10 years in the simple way (rate × area × years). (b) A 20 ha seagrass meadow (illustration) declines 7% per year; calculate the area after 5 years, then compare with a rate of 0.9% per year. (c) Write two sentences concluding how much of the loss can still be prevented.

### Answer key

1. (a) 900 ÷ 30 = 30 ha per year. (b) 900 ÷ 20 = 45 ha per year. (c) (296 − 158) ÷ 296 = 138 ÷ 296 = 46.6%.
2. Rate 1%: 564 × 0.99^15 = 564 × 0.8601 = 485.1 ha; lost 78.9 ha. Rate 2%: 564 × 0.98^15 = 564 × 0.7386 = 416.6 ha; lost 147.4 ha. Doubling the rate makes the loss 1.87 times, not twice, because the base area gets smaller.
3. (a) 8 × 9.6 = 76.8 ha to 8 × 180 = 1,440 ha. (b) At most 564 ÷ 9.6 = 58.75 ha (lowest requirement); at least 564 ÷ 180 = 3.13 ha (highest requirement). An 8 ha pond already exceeds the upper limit if the forest requirement is 180 ha per hectare of pond.
4. Stratum B: shallow subsidence = 5.0 − 4.2 = 0.8 mm per year. Gap at 3.4 mm per year: 3.4 − 4.2 = −0.8 (the soil surface rises faster than sea level); 80 years = −64 mm. At 9.1: 9.1 − 4.2 = 4.9 mm per year, 392 mm in 80 years. Stratum C: shallow subsidence = 3.0 − 1.5 = 1.5 mm per year. Gap at 3.4: 1.9 mm per year, 152 mm in 80 years. At 9.1: 7.6 mm per year, 608 mm in 80 years. Stratum C is the most vulnerable because its surface height change is the smallest (1.5 mm per year). Stratum B is only safe at a rate of 3.4 mm per year.
5. (a) Climate rate 0.1% to 0.4% per year. In 10 years: 564 × 0.001 × 10 = 5.64 ha to 564 × 0.004 × 10 = 22.56 ha. (b) 20 × 0.93^5 = 13.9 ha (lost 6.1 ha); at 0.9%: 20 × 0.991^5 = 19.1 ha. (c) Example answer: mangrove loss directly caused by climate is only 10 to 20% of the loss from human activities, so most of the loss can still be prevented. For seagrass, the causes are dredging, sediment, and nutrients, which can also be controlled (H chapter 12).

## Find the error

Check the two short reports below. Both look plausible, but each contains an error.

**Report A (illustration).** "A 10 ha restoration site was planted with Rhizophora at the start of this year. The site's carbon stock was calculated with the total stock of Sample Forest stratum B: 670 Mg C/ha × 10 ha = 6,700 Mg C. The site is immediately reported to store 6,700 Mg C."

**Report B (illustration).** "In stratum A, accretion measured with marker horizons is 6.0 mm per year, while sea level rise is 3.4 mm per year. Because accretion is greater than sea level rise, stratum A is declared able to keep pace with sea level rise."

### Key

**Report A.** The error: the mature forest stock was used for a newly planted site. A restoration site does not have a significant soil carbon store for the first few years (Module 7; BC chapter 3), and newly planted trees do not have biomass like the mature stratum B stand. The figure of 6,700 Mg C has no measurement basis. The correct way: measure the initial stock in monitoring plots at that site (Modules 7 and 8), then report the stock change at re-measurement, and cite stratum B only as a comparison target. How to detect it: check the age of the stand at the site and ask which plot the per-hectare figure came from.

**Report B.** The error: accretion was compared directly with sea level rise. What matters is the soil surface height change from SET, because part of accretion is lost as shallow subsidence (decaying roots). With the stratum A data: shallow subsidence = 6.0 − 2.5 = 3.5 mm per year, so surface height only rises 2.5 mm per year, smaller than 3.4 mm per year. The gap is 0.9 mm per year, or 72 mm in 80 years; the conclusion "able to keep pace" is wrong. How to detect it: make sure the two rates compared are the rate of surface height change (SET) and sea level, with the same unit (mm per year); ask for the SET value in the report.

## The role of AI and example prompts

AI is used to draft formulas, explain terms, and check calculations. AI is not used as a source of numbers. Every rate, area, and quotation must be matched against the books (H, BC, KD) and recalculated by hand.

**Prompt 1: drafting a remaining-area formula.**

```markdown
In Excel, cell B2 holds the initial area (ha), B3 the loss rate per year (in percent), B4 the number of years.
Write a formula for the remaining area with compound calculation in B5, and a formula for the area lost in B6.
Explain each part of the formula in one sentence.
```

Check: enter 564, 0.66%, and 10. The results must be 527.9 ha (B5) and 36.1 ha (B6), the same as Worked example 3. Change B4 to 1; B5 must equal 564 × (1 − 0.0066) = 560.3 ha. If cell B3 is formatted as a percent, make sure its value is 0.0066, not 0.66.

**Prompt 2: sea level balance formulas.**

```markdown
I have a table with column A stratum, B accretion (mm/year), C surface height change from SET (mm/year),
and cell F1 holds the sea level rate (mm/year). Write formulas for column D (shallow subsidence = accretion minus SET),
column E (gap = sea level minus SET), and column G containing "falling behind" if E is greater than 0, otherwise "keeping pace".
```

Check: for stratum A (6.0; 2.5; F1 = 3.4) the results must be D = 3.5; E = 0.9; G = "falling behind". For stratum B (5.0; 4.2) the results must be D = 0.8; E = −0.8; G = "keeping pace". Make sure cell F1 is locked with a dollar sign when the formula is copied down (the form `$F$1`).

**Prompt 3: checking a quoted number.**

```markdown
I wrote: "Mangroves attenuate waves by up to 66% after 100 m of forest." From which chapter of Hogarth's book does this number come,
and what conditions does the author state (species, size, density)? If you are not sure, say you are not sure.
```

Check: open H 11.1.1 and match the number and the conditions. If the AI names another chapter or number, use what is written in the book and record the difference.

**Example of an AI answer that can be wrong.** Suppose the AI answers: "A 2.0 m storm wave passes through 1.5 km of mangrove. Every 100 m attenuates 66%, so the final height = 2.0 × (1 − 0.66)^15 = practically zero. The forest eliminates the wave completely."

How to catch it:

1. Recalculate: 2.0 × 0.34^15 is less than 0.001 m. The calculation is arithmetically correct, but its underlying assumption is the problem.
2. Match with the book. The figure of 66% is an upper bound "after 100 m", not a rate that repeats every 100 m, and for storm waves the book gives a decrease of 40 to 50 cm per kilometre (H 11.1.1). In the same example the result is 1.25 to 1.40 m (Worked example 2).
3. Reject the conclusion "eliminates the wave completely" because it is not in the book, and ask the AI to state the assumptions used.

In Excel or Sheets in Indonesian, function names and argument separators differ: IF becomes JIKA, ROUND becomes BULATKAN, SUM becomes JUMLAH, SUMPRODUCT becomes SUMPRODUK, and the argument separator is a semicolon (for example `=JIKA(E2>0;"falling behind";"keeping pace")`). ## Weekly assignment

The week 8 assignment has two parts: submit the final project and write a short reflection. The format, template, and rubric of the final project follow the Final project tab; this assignment only checks completeness and its link to this module. Output of the week: the final project submitted.

**Pass criteria**

- [ ] The final project is submitted complete according to the Final project tab (all requested workbook sheets are filled in).
- [ ] Every number that comes from a book carries a reference (H, BC, or KD) and every invented number is labeled "illustration".
- [ ] Where numbers differ between books, the number used is written down together with its book.
- [ ] The reported carbon stock comes with uncertainty (95% confidence interval) and the unit Mg C/ha or Mg CO2e.
- [ ] Threats section: at least two threats relevant to the project site are written, with the affected carbon pools.
- [ ] Restoration or management section: if the project includes a restoration site, the soil carbon stock of the new site is not equated with mature forest (Report A in "Find the error").

- [ ] All calculations in the project have been recalculated by hand once, and AI use (if any) is recorded with its checking steps.
- [ ] A short reflection of 150 to 250 words is written: the three things that most changed how the participant views coastal carbon stocks, one limitation of the project data, and one next step.
